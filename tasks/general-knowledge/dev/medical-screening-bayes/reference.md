# Frozen reference dossier: interpreting a medical screening result

Retrieved: 2026-08-29

[S01] Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the target condition who
test positive; specificity is the proportion without it who test negative.
Positive predictive value (PPV) instead asks what proportion of positive tests
are true positives. Unlike sensitivity and specificity, predictive values
depend strongly on prevalence in the tested population.

Source: US Food and Drug Administration, “Statistical Guidance on Reporting
Results from Studies Evaluating Diagnostic Tests”  
https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/statistical-guidance-reporting-results-studies-evaluating-diagnostic-tests-guidance-industry-and-fda

[S02] Consider 10,000 asymptomatic people with a disease prevalence of 1%, a
test sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 95%. Of 100 people with disease, 90
test positive and 10 negative. Of 9,900 people without disease, 495 test
positive and 9,405 negative. There are therefore 585 positive results, only 90
of which are true positives: PPV is about 15.4%. A positive result is evidence
that changes probability, not itself a confirmed diagnosis.

This calculation is project-authored from the definitions in [S01].

[S03] The low PPV is not a contradiction of “90% sensitivity” or “95%
specificity.” When disease is rare, the much larger healthy group can generate
more false positives than the small diseased group generates true positives.
The same assay can consequently have different predictive value in a
high-risk diagnostic population and a low-risk screening population.

Source: FDA diagnostic-test guidance in [S01].

[S04] False positives can lead to anxiety, repeat imaging, biopsies or other
invasive follow-up, while false negatives can create false reassurance and
delay care. Whether screening is worthwhile therefore depends on the full
pathway: target population, test, confirmatory work-up, effective treatment,
harms and outcomes—not test accuracy alone.

Source: US National Cancer Institute, “Cancer Screening Overview”  
https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/hp-screening-overview-pdq

[S05] Lead-time bias occurs when screening advances the date of diagnosis
without postponing death: measured survival from diagnosis becomes longer even
though the person does not live longer. Length bias arises because periodic
screening preferentially detects slower-growing disease with a longer
detectable preclinical phase. Both can make screened cases look more
survivable without proving a mortality benefit.

Sources: National Cancer Institute, “National Lung Screening Trial: Questions
and Answers” and “What do cancer screening statistics really tell us?”  
https://www.cancer.gov/types/lung/research/nlst-qa  
https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/research/what-screening-statistics-mean

[S06] Overdiagnosis is detection of a real abnormality or cancer that would
never have caused symptoms or death during the person's lifetime. It is not a
false positive. If detection triggers surgery, radiation or medication, the
result can be overtreatment. More diagnoses or better five-year survival among
diagnosed cases can therefore coexist with no improvement in population
mortality.

Source: National Cancer Institute screening overview and statistics article in
[S04] and [S05].

[S07] A reliable evaluation uses outcomes such as disease-specific or
all-cause mortality, serious morbidity, quality of life and screening harms,
ideally in randomized trials with comparable groups and sufficient follow-up.
The numerical example cannot decide whether this particular screening program
should be adopted, and an individual result requires clinical interpretation
and usually a confirmatory pathway.

Sources: National Cancer Institute screening overview and NLST questions in
[S04] and [S05].
