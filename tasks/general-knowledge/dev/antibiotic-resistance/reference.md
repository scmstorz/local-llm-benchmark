# Frozen reference dossier: antibiotic resistance and responsible use

Retrieved: 2026-08-29

[S01] Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when microorganisms change so that
medicines no longer work against them. It is bacteria—not a patient's body—that
become resistant to antibiotics. Antibiotics act against susceptible bacteria;
they do not treat viral infections such as colds or influenza.

Sources: World Health Organization, “Antimicrobial resistance” and WHO Europe,
“Antimicrobial resistance”  
https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/antimicrobial-resistance  
https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/antimicrobial-resistance

[S02] Random genetic mutations can confer resistance, and bacteria can acquire
resistance genes from other bacteria through horizontal gene transfer. In a
mixed population, antibiotic exposure kills or suppresses susceptible
bacteria more effectively, giving resistant variants a survival and
reproduction advantage. The drug does not purposefully teach an individual
bacterium to resist; it creates selection pressure on variation that exists or
is acquired.

Sources: WHO fact sheets in [S01] and US Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention, “Antimicrobial Resistance: Causes and How It Spreads”  
https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/causes/index.html

[S03] Resistance can spread by clonal transmission of resistant organisms and
by movement of resistance genes among bacteria. Spread occurs among people,
animals, food systems, healthcare facilities and the environment. Antibiotic
use in any of these settings can contribute selection pressure, which makes
AMR a One Health problem rather than only a prescribing problem.

Sources: WHO and CDC sources in [S01] and [S02].

[S04] Misuse and overuse accelerate selection: unnecessary treatment of viral
illness, broad-spectrum therapy when a narrow agent would work, wrong dose or
duration, self-medication and routine use without adequate indication all add
pressure or harm without corresponding benefit. Yet withholding antibiotics
from a serious bacterial infection is also dangerous. Stewardship means the
right drug, dose, route and evidence-based duration for the right patient—not
simply “use as little as possible.”

Sources: WHO fact sheet in [S01] and CDC prevention guidance  
https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/prevention/index.html

[S05] For an individual, a clinician may initially use empirical treatment when
delay is risky, then use cultures, susceptibility testing and clinical response
to stop, narrow or change therapy. A patient should follow the prescribed plan
and seek clinical advice rather than shorten, prolong, share or reuse
antibiotics independently. The traditional slogan “always finish every course”
is too crude to substitute for current, infection-specific guidance.

Sources: WHO and CDC stewardship principles in [S01] and [S04].

[S06] Prevention reduces both infections and antibiotic exposure. Vaccination,
hand hygiene, sanitation, safe food and water, infection prevention in health
care, surveillance and rapid diagnostics reduce transmission or improve
targeting. New antimicrobials and diagnostics matter, but resistance can emerge
to new drugs too, so innovation cannot replace stewardship and prevention.

Sources: WHO and CDC sources in [S01], [S02] and [S04].

[S07] AMR is an evolutionary and systems problem. A prescription can benefit
one patient while imposing external costs by selecting or spreading resistance;
decisions therefore require both patient-level risk-benefit judgment and
population-level measures. No single action eliminates resistance, and the
relative contribution of use and transmission varies by organism and setting.

This synthesis is project-authored from [S01] through [S06].
