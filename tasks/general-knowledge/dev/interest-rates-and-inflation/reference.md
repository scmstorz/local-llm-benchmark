# Frozen reference dossier: interest rates and inflation

Retrieved: 2026-08-28

[S01] A central bank directly sets or closely controls a short-term policy
interest rate, not every interest rate and not the price level itself. Policy
and expected future policy affect money-market rates, bond yields, bank funding
costs and ultimately borrowing and saving rates faced by households and firms.
Financial-market reactions can be fast, but pass-through varies by instrument,
contract and banking conditions.

[S02] Higher borrowing costs and a greater return to saving tend to restrain
interest-sensitive household consumption, housing and business investment.
Tighter financial conditions and lower asset values can reinforce this. The
resulting slowdown in aggregate demand reduces pressure on productive capacity
and labor markets, which over time can slow wage and price increases.

[S03] Monetary policy also works through expectations and exchange rates.
Credible expectations of tighter policy can affect longer-term interest rates
and price- or wage-setting before the full demand effect arrives. Other things
equal, a currency appreciation can make imports cheaper, although exchange
rates respond to many forces and the direction or size is not guaranteed in a
particular episode.

[S04] The transmission is neither direct nor immediate. Effects on real
activity and inflation arrive with long, variable and uncertain lags; estimates
often place important or maximum effects roughly one to two years after a
policy change, but timing and strength depend on the economic environment.

[S05] Disinflation through weaker demand has costs and distributional effects.
Output and employment can be lower than otherwise, debt service rises for some
borrowers, and savers or lenders can benefit. Central banks therefore face a
trade-off between returning inflation toward target and avoiding unnecessary
damage to activity, especially when the inflation source may fade by itself.

[S06] Higher interest rates cannot directly produce more gas, oil,
semiconductors or shipping capacity, so they do not repair the physical cause
of a supply shock. Tightening can nevertheless limit second-round effects by
preventing temporarily higher prices from becoming embedded in generalized
demand, wages, price-setting and inflation expectations. Responding too
aggressively to a temporary supply shock can depress employment after the
original shock has already faded; persistence and expectations therefore
matter.

Source: Federal Reserve speech, “Headline versus Core Inflation in the Conduct
of Monetary Policy”  
https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/mishkin20071020a.htm

[S07] Simple example: if mortgage, auto-loan and business-financing rates rise
after a policy-rate increase, some purchases and investments are postponed.
With less demand growth, firms may find it harder to raise prices and hiring may
cool. This is a transmission chain with uncertain size and delay, not a switch
that mechanically cuts every price.
